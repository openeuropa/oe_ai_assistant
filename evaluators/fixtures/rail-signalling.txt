One signalling system for European railways

Europe's railways were built as national networks, and nothing shows that more plainly than their
signalling. Each country developed its own system for telling a driver how fast to go and when to
stop, with its own trackside equipment, its own onboard reader and its own logic. More than twenty
incompatible systems remain in service. A locomotive crossing three borders historically needed
three sets of equipment and a driver qualified on all three, which is why international trains were
so often handed over at the frontier.

The European Rail Traffic Management System is the agreed replacement, and its train control
component, the European Train Control System, works on a different principle from most of what it
supersedes. Instead of signals at the lineside that a driver reads, the train is given a movement
authority: permission to proceed to a defined point at defined speeds. The onboard computer
compares that authority against the train's braking characteristics and supervises the driver
continuously, intervening if the train would overrun it.

Deployment is described in levels, and the levels are not a quality ranking but a description of how
much lineside equipment remains. At the first level the existing signals stay and balises in the
track pass the authority to the train at fixed points. At the second, authorities arrive by radio
from a control centre and the lineside signals can come down, which removes the hardware that costs
most to maintain. Higher levels dispense with trackside train detection as well, letting the train
report its own position and integrity.

The hard part has never been the specification. It is that a line must carry traffic throughout the
transition. Fitting a route means equipping the infrastructure and every train that uses it,
including those of operators from other countries, and until the last of them is fitted the old
system has to stay in place too. Dual fitting is expensive, and the economic case for a route
arrives only when the old equipment can finally be switched off. That sequencing problem, rather
than any technical doubt, explains why deployment has taken decades and why national plans are
coordinated through binding timetables for the core network corridors.

Interoperability depends on more than signalling, and the technical specifications cover the rest:
the loading gauge that fixes how large a vehicle may be, the electrification systems a locomotive
must handle, the couplers, the brakes. A vehicle authorised in one member state is now authorised
once for an area of use that can span several, with the European Union Agency for Railways issuing
the authorisation rather than each national authority in turn.

The benefit is easiest to see in freight. A train from a North Sea port to northern Italy crosses
several networks, and every change of system is a point at which the locomotive, the driver or both
must be exchanged, adding hours to a journey that competes with road haulage on exactly that margin.
Removing those breaks is the practical argument for the whole programme, and it is why the corridors
carrying the most freight were fitted first.
