Working in twenty-four languages

The European Union has twenty-four official languages, and the rule that put them there is almost as
old as the institutions themselves: Regulation No 1 of 1958, the first regulation the Council ever
adopted. Its consequence is unusual among international organisations. Legislation is not translated
into other languages after being agreed in one. Every language version of a legal act is equally
authentic, which means no version is the original and none can be treated as a translation of
another.

That principle shapes how law is drafted. A text that is clear in one language and ambiguous in
another is a defect to be fixed before adoption, not after, so lawyer-linguists work on draft acts
alongside the lawyers who write them, checking that each version carries the same legal meaning
rather than the same words. Where a concept exists in one national legal tradition and not in
another, the drafters must either find a formulation that works in both or define the term in the
act itself. Much of the Union's characteristic vocabulary exists for that reason.

Interpreting is a separate discipline with separate constraints. Speech at a meeting is simultaneous
and cannot be revised, and with twenty-four languages the number of possible language pairs runs
into the hundreds, far more than any booth can staff directly. The answer is relay interpreting: an
interpreter working from a less widely covered language into a pivot language, from which colleagues
take it onward. Relay adds a few seconds of delay and a second point at which nuance can be lost,
which is why interpreters ask speakers to supply written texts in advance and to resist reading them
at speed.

Not every document is translated. Legal acts, official correspondence with a member state and
documents addressed to the public are, because the right to deal with the institutions in one's own
language is the reason the regime exists. Internal working papers are usually drafted and circulated
in a smaller set of procedural languages, and the practical dominance of English in that internal
layer coexists with full multilingualism in the published output.

Machine translation changed the volume question without settling the quality one. The institutions
run their own trained systems, and raw output is used for getting the gist of an incoming document
quickly. Anything that will be published, and certainly anything with legal effect, is post-edited
by a translator who remains responsible for it. The distinction holds because a mistranslated
legislative provision is not a drafting inconvenience: it is twenty-four texts that no longer say
the same thing, binding in twenty-seven member states.

Enlargement adds languages, and each addition is a substantial undertaking: terminology has to be
built, the existing body of law has to be translated into the new language before accession, and a
cohort of translators and interpreters has to be trained in a specialism that few universities
teach. The cost of all of it is frequently raised and routinely defended on the same ground the 1958
regulation assumed, that law binding citizens should be readable by them.
