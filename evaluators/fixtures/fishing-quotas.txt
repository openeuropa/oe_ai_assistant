How fishing quotas are set under the Common Fisheries Policy

Every December, fisheries ministers from the member states meet in Brussels to fix the total
allowable catch for the following year, stock by stock. The meeting is the visible end of a process
that has been running since the previous spring, and almost nothing decided in the room is invented
there. What ministers negotiate is the gap between what the scientific advice recommends and what
each national fleet can absorb.

The advice itself comes from the International Council for the Exploration of the Sea, which is not
a European Union body and advises coastal states across the North Atlantic. Its working groups
assemble catch records, research survey data and biological sampling into an assessment of each
stock, and express the result as the catch consistent with maximum sustainable yield. That concept
is the policy's legal objective: the largest quantity that can be taken year after year without
reducing the stock's ability to replace itself. Where data are too thin for a full assessment, the
advice falls back on a precautionary approach and recommends more cautious limits.

The Commission turns that advice into a proposal, and this is where the arithmetic becomes
political. A single trawl rarely catches a single species, so a quota cut on one stock can stop a
fleet from catching its full entitlement on another that shares the same ground. Reducing a catch
limit to the level the science suggests may therefore tie up vessels that depend on a mixed fishery,
and ministers who represent those fleets argue for a slower path to the target.

Allocation between member states is the part that does not get renegotiated. Each state's share of a
stock is fixed by a distribution key known as relative stability, set from historic catch records in
the early years of the policy and left deliberately untouched since. A state that wants more of one
stock and less of another trades during the year through quota swaps, which are bilateral,
administratively routine, and invisible in the December figures.

The landing obligation changed what a quota means. Before it, a vessel that exceeded its quota for
one species while fishing for another discarded the excess at sea, dead and unrecorded. The
obligation requires those catches to be brought ashore and counted, which makes the limiting stock
in a mixed fishery decisive: once its quota is exhausted, the vessel must stop fishing even if its
other quotas are untouched. That consequence, often called the choke species problem, drives much of
the technical argument over gear selectivity and over the exemptions that soften it.

Stocks shared with states outside the Union follow a parallel track. Catch limits for stocks that
straddle the boundary with Norway, the United Kingdom, Iceland or the Faroe Islands are agreed in
bilateral or coastal state consultations before the December Council, and the Union's internal
allocation then applies to its agreed share. Where those consultations fail, as they have
periodically for mackerel and for blue whiting, each party sets its own limit and the sum exceeds
the scientific advice, which is the clearest illustration of why the negotiation matters as much as
the assessment.
